Chuseok — also called *Korean Thanksgiving* — is hands-down the holiday that explains modern Korea better than any other, and if you happen to be here during the three-day celebration, you'll witness the country transform in ways no guidebook fully captures. Every autumn, roughly **50 million people** simultaneously try to return to their hometowns, eat mountains of handmade *songpyeon* rice cakes, bow before ancestral altars, and stare at the brightest full moon of the year. Having lived in Seoul for several years and experienced the chaos firsthand, I can tell you: it's overwhelming, moving, delicious, and absolutely worth understanding before you arrive.

---

## What Is Chuseok? Korea's Harvest Moon Holiday Defined

Chuseok (추석) is a three-day national holiday rooted in an ancient harvest festival, falling on the **15th day of the 8th lunar month** — which in 2026 lands on **October 2 (Friday)**, with the public holiday window running from October 1–3. The word itself is often translated as "Autumn Eve," and the holiday traces back at least to the Silla dynasty (57 BCE–935 CE), when communities gathered to give thanks for a bountiful harvest and honor their ancestors.

Think of it as the Korean equivalent of Thanksgiving and the Mid-Autumn Festival rolled into one, but with its own entirely distinct rituals. According to [Korea.net](https://www.korea.net/AboutKorea/Korean-Life/Chuseok), the holiday is officially designated as one of Korea's two major명절 (*myeongjeol*, or national folk holidays), the other being Lunar New Year (*Seollal*). Families gather, prepare food together, perform *charye* (차례) — a Confucian ancestral memorial rite held at dawn on Chuseok morning — and then visit family graves to tidy them up in a practice called *beolcho* (벌초).

What strikes most international visitors is the sheer emotional weight of the holiday. It isn't just a long weekend; it's the one time of year when Korean families, no matter how scattered, are expected to be together.

## The Great Korean Traffic Migration: What to Expect on the Roads

Every Chuseok, Korea experiences one of the world's most concentrated mass migrations — and the roads tell the whole story. During the 2025 Chuseok holiday period, the Korea Expressway Corporation reported that **daily expressway traffic peaked at over 5.9 million vehicles**, with Seoul-to-Busan travel times stretching to **seven or eight hours** on what is normally a four-hour drive (as of September 2025, [Korea Expressway Corporation](https://www.ex.co.kr)).

Here's the honest breakdown of what to expect:

| Travel Direction | Worst Day | Approximate Delay |
|---|---|---|
| Out of Seoul (southbound/eastbound) | Eve of Chuseok (Oct 1) | +3–5 hrs vs. normal |
| Into Seoul (northbound) | Last day of holiday (Oct 3) | +3–5 hrs vs. normal |
| KTX (high-speed rail) | All three days | Fully booked weeks in advance |
| Express buses | All three days | Sell out within hours of opening |

My personal tip: **KTX tickets open 30 days in advance** through [Korail](https://www.letskorail.com), and they vanish within minutes on opening day. If you're planning to travel anywhere by train during Chuseok 2026, set an alarm for the exact minute booking opens in early September. Alternatively, embrace the holiday in Seoul — the city is eerily quiet, museums often run special programmes, and the Han River parks are genuinely peaceful.

If you have no choice but to drive, leave before 6 a.m. or after 10 p.m. on peak days. The traffic apps *Kakao Map* and *Naver Map* give real-time expressway conditions that are genuinely accurate.

## Songpyeon and the Chuseok Feast: A Food Lover's Guide

The undisputed star of Chuseok cuisine is **songpyeon** (송편) — half-moon-shaped rice cakes stuffed with sweet fillings like sesame seeds, red bean paste, or chestnut, then steamed over a bed of pine needles that give them a faint, resinous fragrance. Making songpyeon together the night before Chuseok is a family ritual in itself; Korean lore says that whoever shapes the prettiest songpyeon will give birth to a beautiful child or meet a handsome partner. (I tried making them once, and mine looked like lumpy UFOs — make of that what you will.)

Beyond songpyeon, the Chuseok table — called *charye sang* (차례상) — is an elaborate spread. Traditional dishes include:

- **Japchae** (잡채) — glass noodles stir-fried with vegetables and beef
- **Jeon** (전) — pan-fried savoury pancakes with fish, vegetables, or meat
- **Galbijjim** (갈비찜) — braised short ribs slow-cooked in a sweet-savoury sauce
- **Sikhye** (식혜) — a sweet fermented rice punch served as a digestif
- **Taro soup** (*toranguk*, 토란국) — a Chuseok-specific autumn soup

The exact dishes vary by region and family. Families in the Jeolla region tend to be particularly famous for the variety and richness of their *jeon*, while Gyeongsang families might emphasize different fish preparations. What's universal is the sheer volume of food and the hours — sometimes an entire day — that go into preparing it.

If you're not lucky enough to be invited to a Korean home this Chuseok, many traditional restaurants and hotel buffets run special Chuseok menus. Department store basement food halls (*sikpum-gwan*) also stock gorgeous pre-made *jeon* and *galbi* sets in the week leading up to the holiday — expect long queues but high quality. [VisitKorea](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr) regularly updates its festival and restaurant listings around the holiday season, so that's worth bookmarking.

## Ancestral Rites, Full Moon Customs, and Regional Traditions

Chuseok is as much a spiritual occasion as a culinary one — and understanding the rituals helps you appreciate why it matters so deeply to Koreans. On the morning of Chuseok, families perform *charye*, a table-based ancestral rite where food is laid out in a specific order and family members bow in even numbers (two bows for the living, four for the deceased) to honor their ancestors. It's a solemn but warm ceremony that typically takes about 30–60 minutes, after which the family eats the offered food together.

Later in the day, many families travel to ancestral graves to clear weeds and tidy the site (*seongmyo*, 성묘). In rural areas, you'll see these grassy hillside mounds carefully maintained; in cities, columbarium visits are increasingly common.

Come nightfall, the tradition of *ganggangsullae* (강강술래) — a circular dance performed under the harvest moon — is one of the most visually stunning Chuseok customs. Originally a folk dance from South Jeolla Province, it's now recognized as a **UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage** (inscribed 2009). Women in *hanbok* join hands and sing while dancing in an ever-quickening circle beneath the full moon. You can catch public performances at folk villages and cultural centres nationwide during the holiday.

The full moon itself — called *hangawi dal* (한가위달) — is a big deal. Families gather on rooftops, in parks, or at mountain ridges to watch it rise and make wishes. If you're in Korea on Chuseok night, find a high spot with a clear eastern view. The moon really does look different, or maybe it just feels that way.

## Chuseok 2026: Practical Survival Guide for Visitors and Expats

Being in Korea during Chuseok as a foreigner is a genuinely special experience — but it requires planning, because the holiday operates by its own rules. Here's what you actually need to know:

**What's closed:**
Most independent restaurants, small shops, and local markets are **closed for all three days** (and sometimes the day before). Convenience stores (GS25, CU, 7-Eleven) stay open. Major department stores like Lotte and Shinsegae typically open on at least two of the three days — check their apps closer to the date, as policies change annually.

**What's open and worth visiting:**
- **National Folk Museum of Korea** (Gyeongbokgung complex) — runs free Chuseok cultural programmes including *hanbok* try-ons and traditional games like *yutnori*
- **Korean Folk Village in Yongin** — one of the best spots to experience a full traditional Chuseok celebration; check [Korean Folk Village](https://www.koreanfolk.co.kr) for their 2026 programme schedule
- **Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung palaces** — free entry in *hanbok* on all three days (policy as of August 2026; confirm on-site)
- **Han River parks** — free, beautiful, and full of families

**Quick checklist before Chuseok:**
1. Stock up on groceries 2–3 days before the holiday.
2. Book KTX/buses the moment tickets open (30 days prior).
3. Withdraw cash — some smaller vendors go card-free during the rush.
4. Download Kakao Map for real-time traffic.
5. Check restaurant/museum hours individually; don't assume anything is open.
6. If invited to a Korean home, bring a premium food gift set (*seontul*) — premium nuts, oils, or Korean beef sets are all appropriate.

Prices and schedules change every year, so always verify details on [VisitKorea's official English site](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr) closer to the date.

## Key Takeaways: Everything You Need to Remember About Chuseok

Chuseok is the heartbeat of Korean culture — a holiday that compresses the country's reverence for family, ancestors, food, and the harvest moon into three intense, beautiful days. **In 2026, it falls on October 1–3**, with October 2 as the official holiday. Roads will be jammed, trains fully booked, and most small businesses closed — but in their place, you get one of the most authentic windows into Korean life that exists.

Whether you're sampling *songpyeon* at a folk village, watching the full moon rise over a Seoul palace, or just marvelling at the emptied streets of a city that's usually in perpetual motion, Chuseok is the kind of experience that stays with you long after you fly home.

**Quick recap:**
- 📅 **Date:** October 1–3, 2026 (full moon on October 2)
- 🍡 **Must-eat:** Songpyeon, jeon, galbijjim
- 🚗 **Traffic peak:** October 1 (outbound) and October 3 (inbound)
- 🎎 **Best cultural spots:** National Folk Museum, Korean Folk Village, palace complexes
- 📦 **Gift etiquette:** Premium food sets are the go-to *seontul*
