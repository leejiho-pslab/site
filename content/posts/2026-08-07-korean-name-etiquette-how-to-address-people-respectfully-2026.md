Korean name etiquette and honorifics are at the heart of Korean social norms — and getting them right will instantly earn you respect in any setting in 2026. I still remember the first time I called my Korean colleague by just his first name in a team meeting. The brief, polite silence that followed said everything. This guide gives you the full picture: how Korean names work, what titles and honorifics to use, how social norms are quietly evolving, and exactly what to say (and avoid) so you never cause that awkward pause again.

## How Korean Names Are Structured — and Why It Matters

**Korean names follow a family name–first order**, which is the single most important thing to understand before anything else. In Korean, the family name (성, *seong*) comes first, followed by the given name (이름, *ireum*), which is usually two syllables. So in the name **Kim Jisoo (김지수)**, "Kim" is the family name and "Jisoo" is the given name — the reverse of Western convention.

A few things that surprised me when I moved here:

- **About 21% of all Koreans share the surname Kim**, with Lee (이) and Park (박) accounting for another large chunk, as of August 2026 according to Statistics Korea ([kostat.go.kr](https://kostat.go.kr)). This means family names alone are almost useless for identifying someone; given names carry much more individual weight.
- Given names are often chosen for their hanja (Chinese character) meaning — parents spend serious time on this, sometimes consulting a fortune-teller or naming specialist.
- Some Koreans who frequently interact internationally adopt an English name for convenience, but **never assume this and never assign one for them**.

When someone gives you their name in a Korean context, always note the order. On business cards and official Korean documents, it's family name first. On many English-language Korean business cards, it may be flipped to Western order — but not always, so when in doubt, politely ask.

## Korean Honorifics 101: The Titles You Actually Need to Know

**Korean honorifics (존댓말, *jondaemal*) are not optional extras — they are built into the grammar and culture of every interaction.** Unlike English, where "you" is a one-size-fits-all pronoun, Korean has entirely different speech levels depending on the relationship, age, and social context.

Here are the essential titles and terms of address for everyday situations:

| Context | Term / Title | Romanization | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| General polite "you" | 선생님 | *seonsaengnim* | Lit. "teacher"; used broadly as a respectful address |
| Colleague older than you | 선배님 | *seonbaenim* | Senior at work or school |
| Colleague your age/younger | 동료 / name + 씨 | *dongnyo* / name + *ssi* | *Ssi* (씨) after a full name is neutral-polite |
| Married/older woman | 여사님 | *yeosanim* | Formal, respectful |
| Boss / superior | 부장님, 팀장님, etc. | *bujangnim*, *timjangnim* | Job title + *nim* suffix |
| Stranger (general) | 저기요 | *jeogiyo* | Lit. "over there" — used to get attention politely |
| Older man (informal) | 아저씨 | *ajeossi* | Can feel rude if the person is younger than they look — use carefully |
| Older woman (informal) | 아줌마 / 아주머니 | *ajumma* / *ajumeoni* | *Ajumeoni* is more polite |

The **suffix *nim* (님)** is your best friend. Attach it to almost any title — teacher (선생), doctor (의사), director (감독) — and you instantly sound respectful. I mentally call it the "courtesy upgrade button."

## First Names, Full Names, and the *Ssi* Rule

**Using someone's bare first name is far more intimate in Korea than in most Western cultures** — and using it too early can come across as either rude or overly familiar, depending on the situation.

Here's a practical framework I use:

1. **On first meeting a peer:** Use their full name + *ssi* (씨). For example, *"Jisoo-ssi"* or *"Kim Jisoo-ssi."* This is neutral, polite, and safe in virtually any adult context.
2. **In a professional hierarchy:** Drop *ssi* entirely and use their job title + *nim*. Calling your team leader *"Choi Jungmin-ssi"* when you should be saying *"팀장님 (Timjangnim)"* is a subtle but real faux pas.
3. **With close friends of similar age:** First name alone becomes acceptable once the relationship is established — but let *them* signal when that transition happens, not you.
4. **Never use a senior person's first name alone** unless they've explicitly invited you to. Even then, many Koreans feel uncomfortable doing this themselves.

One nuance worth knowing: **in K-dramas and K-pop, you often hear older people called by their first name by very close friends or romantic partners** — but this is context-specific and usually signals deep intimacy, not everyday casualness. Real-life social norms are stricter than the screen.

## How Social Norms Around Names Are Evolving in 2026

**Korean workplace culture is gradually shifting, but honorifics and titles are far from disappearing.** A growing number of tech startups and global companies operating in Korea — particularly in the Seongsu and Pangyo tech corridors — have adopted a flatter naming culture, sometimes asking all staff to use English names or to address each other with *ssi* regardless of rank.

Kakao famously piloted an "English name only" internal policy, and several other Korean tech firms have followed with varying degrees of success. That said, **outside these pockets of corporate modernity, traditional honorific norms remain firmly in place** — in schools, government offices, healthcare, retail, and most mid-to-large corporations.

What's also changing is the generational attitude toward *age-based hierarchy*. Gen Z Koreans (born roughly 1997–2012) are more likely to feel uncomfortable enforcing strict seniority-based speech, but they still generally use it out of respect for older people's expectations. Think of it as a system in negotiation, not revolution.

## Common Mistakes Foreigners Make (And How to Avoid Them)

**The most common mistake isn't ignorance of the rules — it's assuming Korean norms mirror your home country's.** Here's what I see trip up foreigners most often:

- **Calling someone by their first name on first meeting.** Even if the person smiles and doesn't correct you, they've noted it. Start with full name + *ssi*.
- **Translating Western informality directly.** In many Western workplaces, using first names signals warmth. In Korea, it can read as disrespect — especially toward someone older or senior.
- **Assuming "you" is always okay.** In formal Korean, directly saying "you" (당신, *dangsin*) to someone can actually sound confrontational. Koreans often address each other by name or title instead of a pronoun.
- **Forgetting that titles shift by context.** Your sunbae (선배, senior) at work might also be your friend outside the office — but they're still your *sunbae*.
- **Over-correcting into awkwardness.** If you make a mistake, a simple *"실례했습니다" (siryehaessseumnida — I was rude/I apologize)* with a small bow goes a very long way.

## Practical Checklist: Korean Name Etiquette at a Glance

Before your next interaction with a Korean colleague, host, or new acquaintance, run through this:

- [ ] **Do I know their family name vs. given name?** (Family name is first in Korean order.)
- [ ] **Am I their peer, junior, or senior?** This determines your speech level.
- [ ] **Do I know their job title?** If yes, use title + *nim* in professional settings.
- [ ] **When in doubt, use full name + *ssi*.** It's almost always safe.
- [ ] **Let them set the tone for informality.** If they want first names, they'll tell you.
- [ ] **Avoid translating Western familiarity directly.** What feels warm in English may feel brash in Korean.
- [ ] **When you mess up, apologize simply and move on.** Koreans are genuinely forgiving of foreigners making honest efforts.

Korean name etiquette isn't about memorizing a rigid rulebook — it's about communicating *respect* through the choices you make. Once I internalized that core idea, the specific rules started clicking into place naturally. Take it one interaction at a time, stay curious, and you'll find that most Koreans are touched — not judgmental — when a foreigner makes a genuine effort.

*Schedules, policies, and corporate naming cultures can change — always check official sources like [Korea.net](https://www.korea.net) and [VisitKorea](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr) for the latest guidance before your trip or assignment.*
